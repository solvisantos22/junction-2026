## Reading copy

Imagine driving a car that obeys you three seconds late. That is every day on Mars. So who do you trust to drive?

We built that day. One rover, one day of sunlight, rocks to collect, and one rule: be home before the battery dies.

Then we handed the keys to nine AI models from three labs. Surely one of them makes it home?

Mars doesn't play fair. A fast one reaches for a rock and misses, by 3 centimetres. No points. 9 tries, 1 rock.

A bigger one drives onto a slope too steep. The rover freezes and forgets its orders. 30 times.

And a flagship: 13 metres from home, 21 samples on board. It will not make it.

Nothing broke. So what went wrong?

Its own report says why: the planner counted time, never the battery. And it played all three maps without checking one result.

The sun. It's the only fuel. This rover drove until its battery was flat.

Our driver saw its battery fall, stopped, and waited for light. So how did all nine do?

On three maps in a row, none beat our driver. The best run came within a hair. Ours brought home 48%, and never lost a rover. So what is it?

Not an AI. A planner: plain code that works out the whole day before the wheels turn. Lucky maps?

So we replayed every model's best program on 300 fresh worlds, on Modal. The planner won 95 times in 100. Can a machine learn it instead?

We trained a small one from scratch: from 8% to 36% on unseen worlds, still short of the planner. Even a model trained to pick the planner's strategy gained less than half a point.

AI can write the code. It can't yet plan the day. Can your agent make it home?
